Gender Education Introduction · Alinggo S.L. Lee

Start here. These exercises warm up the vocabulary and sentence frames Alinggo will need before simulating a full class. Martin plays the role of a confused but curious international student.

Vocabulary Quick-Fire

Tap each word to reveal the definition and example sentence. Practice saying the example aloud naturally — don't just read it, teach it.

Stereotype (n.)
Definition: A fixed idea that many people have about a group — often oversimplified or inaccurate.
Try saying: "The idea that 'boys are strong and girls are gentle' is a common stereotype."
刻板印象 — 對某一群體的固定、概括化的看法
Socialization (n.)
Definition: The process of learning to behave in ways that are considered acceptable by society.
Try saying: "Socialization starts early — through the toys parents buy for us and the stories they read to us."
社會化 — 學習社會認可行為的過程
Representation (n.)
Definition: How something (a person, group, or idea) is shown, described, or portrayed.
Try saying: "Today we're going to analyze the representation of girls in children's picture books."
再現 / 表徵 — 某事物如何被呈現或描繪
Gender Expression (n.)
Definition: How you present yourself outwardly — through clothing, behavior, hairstyle, voice, etc.
Try saying: "Gender expression is about how you present yourself — it can be different from your gender identity."
性別表達 — 透過外在行為和穿著展現自我的方式
SOGIESC (acronym)
Stands for: Sexual Orientation, Gender Identity, Gender Expression, Sex Characteristics
Practice: "SOGIESC is a framework that helps us discuss diversity clearly and respectfully. Each of these four aspects is distinct and independent."
性傾向、性別認同、性別表達、性徵 — 四個不同且獨立的面向

Sentence Frame Drill

These are the TEEMI "Professional Frames" from the curriculum. Practice completing each one aloud. Martin can give a topic prompt, and Alinggo responds using the frame.

Frame 1 — Describing an observation:
"One pattern we noticed in children's literature is that..."
Example completion: "...boys are usually the heroes, while girls wait to be rescued."
Frame 2 — Explaining the impact:
"This matters because it reinforces the idea that..."
Example completion: "...girls should be passive, and boys should take charge."
Frame 3 — Summarizing the theory:
"Therefore, it is evidence of how gender norms are..."
Example completion: "...taught from a very young age through everyday media."

💡 Practice idea: Martin names a topic (e.g., "Disney princesses," "toy aisle colors," "school uniforms") → Alinggo uses all 3 frames in sequence to build a mini-analysis. Repeat 3–4 times with different topics.

This is the main event. Alinggo teaches the W1 lesson as if Martin is an international student in her class. The script below gives structure — but she should speak naturally, not read word-for-word. Chinese support notes are included for when she might want to switch to L1 (≤25%).

⏱ Suggested total time 25 min
Segment 1 — Opening

Welcome & Today's Topic

~3 minutes

Alinggo (Teacher)

"Good morning / afternoon, everyone. Welcome to Week 1 of our Gender Education course. Today we're going to explore a very simple but important question: Where do our ideas about gender come from?"

Alinggo

"Before we start — I want to be clear. This class is not about judgment. It's about seeing patterns. And when we can see patterns, we can understand the structure behind them."

今天不是要批判任何人，而是要學會「看見」那些我們平常沒注意到的模式。
💡 Teaching tip

Make eye contact. Pause after "seeing patterns." This is the thesis of the entire week — let it land.

Segment 2 — Core Concept

Introducing SOGIESC

~7 minutes

Alinggo

"To discuss diversity clearly and respectfully, we use a framework called SOGIESC. Let me break it down for you."

Alinggo

"There are four parts, and the important thing to remember is: they are all separate from each other."

Alinggo — explain each one

"First — Sexual Orientation. This is about who you are attracted to."

"Second — Gender Identity. This is your internal sense of being male, female, or another gender. It's how you feel inside."

"Third — Gender Expression. This is how you show yourself to the world — through your clothes, your behavior, your appearance."

"And fourth — Sex Characteristics. These are your physical traits — like chromosomes and hormones."

💡 Comprehension check

After explaining all four, ask: "Can someone give me an example of gender expression?" or "Which one of these four is about what's on the inside — how you feel?" This forces the student to process, not just listen.

Martin (Student) — challenge prompt

Ask a "confused student" question like: "Wait — isn't gender expression the same as gender identity?" to give Alinggo practice clarifying the distinction.

Segment 3 — Discussion

Invisible Lessons in Stories

~10 minutes

Alinggo

"Now let's think about something personal. I want you to think of a story from your childhood — a book, a movie, a fairy tale. Who was the hero? Who needed to be rescued? Who was brave? Who was gentle?"

Alinggo

"These stories seem innocent, right? But they carry invisible lessons about gender. If we always see boys as heroes and girls as people who need help — what does that teach us?"

這些故事看似無害，但其中隱含的性別訊息會不知不覺地影響我們。
Martin (Student) — response prompts

Try responding as a student would:
• "Hmm, I remember reading... and the girl was always waiting for the prince."
• "In my culture, boys are taught to be strong and not cry."
• Challenge: "But isn't it just a story? Does it really affect us?"

Alinggo — using the sentence frames

This is where to deploy the TEEMI frames naturally:

"One pattern we noticed in children's literature is that boys tend to be active characters while girls are passive."

"This matters because it reinforces the idea that leadership and adventure belong to boys."

"Therefore, it is evidence of how gender norms are taught from a young age — quietly, through stories."

Segment 4 — Closing

Wrap-Up & Reflection

~5 minutes

Alinggo

"So — what did we learn today? Gender messages begin quietly. Through stories. Through colors. Through toys. Through the names we are given."

Alinggo

"Remember: this week is not about judgment. It's about seeing. When we see patterns, we begin to see structure. And when we see structure — we can change it."

💡 Closing tip

End with a question for next week: "Between now and next class, I want you to notice one gender message in your daily life. It could be an ad, a toy, a conversation. Bring it to class next week." This gives the lesson a forward hook.

These are tricky classroom moments Alinggo might face with international students. Martin plays the student; Alinggo must respond in English as the teacher. Tap a scenario to expand it.

Scenario 1
The Skeptical Student
A student pushes back on the relevance of the topic.
Student (Martin)

"I don't understand why we need this class. I already treat everyone equally. Why do we need to study gender?"

What to practice

Alinggo should: (1) validate the student's feeling, (2) reframe the purpose — it's not about blame, it's about awareness. Try: "That's a great point, and I appreciate you saying that. This class isn't about changing who you are — it's about understanding the patterns around us that we might not notice."

Scenario 2
The Vocabulary Confusion
A student mixes up gender identity and gender expression.
Student (Martin)

"So if a boy wears a skirt, that means he identifies as a girl?"

What to practice

This is a common misconception. Alinggo should calmly separate the two concepts: "Not necessarily. Wearing a skirt is about gender expression — how you present yourself. Gender identity is how you feel inside. A boy who wears a skirt might still identify as a boy. These are two different things — that's exactly why the SOGIESC framework is useful."

Scenario 3
The Cultural Pushback
A student says "In my country, this is different."
Student (Martin)

"In my country, these gender roles are just normal. Men work, women take care of the family. It's our culture. Are you saying our culture is wrong?"

What to practice

The hardest one. Alinggo should: (1) respect the culture, (2) separate observation from judgment, (3) redirect to patterns. Try: "Thank you for sharing that. Every culture has its own values, and we respect that. In this class, we're not saying any culture is wrong. We're learning to notice patterns — and to ask questions about where those patterns come from. That's a skill we can all use."

Scenario 4
Explaining a Key Term Simply
A student asks "What does 'socialization' mean? I don't understand."
Student (Martin)

"Sorry, I don't know this word — 'socialization'? Can you explain it more simply?"

What to practice

Alinggo should use a concrete everyday example instead of a textbook definition. Try: "Sure! Think about it this way: when you were a child, someone probably told you 'boys don't cry' or gave you a blue toy instead of a pink one. That's socialization — it's how we learn what society thinks is 'normal' for boys and girls. We learn these rules without even realizing it."

Scenario 5
Live Discussion Facilitation
A student gives a one-word answer. Keep the discussion going.
Student (Martin)

Alinggo asks: "Can you think of a gender stereotype from a childhood story?" Martin answers: "Cinderella."

What to practice

Alinggo should use follow-up questions to draw out more: "Cinderella, great example! So — what message does Cinderella give us about girls? What does she need to do before her life gets better?" ... and then: "And what about the prince? What's his role in the story? Does he have any personality, or is he just 'the rescuer'?" Practice drawing a full 2-minute discussion from a one-word answer.
