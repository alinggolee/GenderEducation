Week 12 Teaching Practice

Gender Education · Alinggo S.L. Lee

Week 12 shifts from bodies to digital systems — how platforms and algorithms amplify gendered harm. The key insight: digital harm is not only caused by bad people; it is caused by how platforms are designed.

Vocabulary Quick-Fire

Algorithm (n.)
Definition: A set of rules a computer follows to decide what you see online — designed to maximize engagement, not safety.
Try saying: "The algorithm prioritized content that got many likes, even if it was harmful — because harmful content drives engagement."
演算法 — 電腦用來決定你看到什麼內容的規則，設計目標是最大化互動，而非安全。
Platform Accountability (n.)
Definition: The responsibility of digital platforms for the content they amplify and the harm their systems enable.
Try saying: "We need more platform accountability — it's not enough to tell users to 'block and report.'"
平台問責 — 數位平台對其放大的內容和系統造成的傷害所應承擔的責任。
Engagement (n.)
Definition: Interaction on social media — likes, shares, comments. Platforms prioritize engagement because it generates data and profit.
Try saying: "Platforms care about engagement because it generates profit — even when that engagement comes from harmful content."
互動 — 社群媒體上的按讚、分享、留言。平台優先追求互動，因為互動產生利潤。
Amplification (n.)
Definition: When a platform's algorithm shows harmful content to more people, making the problem bigger than it would be naturally.
Try saying: "A video that makes fun of girls may get many likes. Then the algorithm amplifies it — showing it to thousands more people."
放大效應 — 演算法將有害內容推送給更多人，使問題規模遠超自然傳播。
Systemic Change (n.)
Definition: Changing the system itself — not just telling individuals to protect themselves, but redesigning how the system works.
Try saying: "We need systemic change, not just personal protection. The question is: why does the system keep allowing this to happen?"
系統性改變 — 不只告訴個人要自我保護，而是改變系統本身的運作方式。

Sentence Frame Drill

Frame 1 — Opening:
"I want to focus on how algorithmic systems affect..."
Frame 2 — Impact:
"Online harm has a disproportionate impact on _____ because..."
Frame 3 — Solution:
"A better strategy would involve structural reform of..."

W12: Digital Gender-Based Violence — How Systems Shape Harm. The lesson moves from platform design → the "digital mirror" → user vs. platform accountability → systemic change.

⏱ Suggested total time30 min
Segment 1 — Opening

It's Not Just Bad People — It's Bad Design

~5 minutes

Alinggo

"Digital harm is not only caused by bad people. It is also caused by how digital platforms are designed. Platforms want you to keep scrolling. Their algorithms show content that gets many likes, comments, and shares. And because of this, shocking or harmful gender content often gets more views."

Alinggo

"Example: a video that makes fun of girls may get many likes. Then the platform shows it to more people. The algorithm amplifies the harm. So the problem becomes bigger — not because more people are bad, but because the system is designed to spread what's engaging, not what's safe."

問題不在於更多人是壞人，而在於系統被設計來傳播有互動的內容，而非安全的內容。
Segment 2 — Discussion

The Digital Mirror & Unequal Impact

~10 minutes

Alinggo

"Have you noticed that your 'For You' page can change how you think about gender? If you often see videos showing girls caring about beauty, you may start thinking that's what girls 'should' do. If you see boys always being strong and not showing feelings, you think that's 'normal.' The algorithm creates a digital mirror that reflects social norms back at you — and reinforces them."

Alinggo

"And digital violence doesn't affect everyone equally. Girls, LGBTQ+ people, and people who look 'different' receive more online attacks. Online problems come from real-life inequality."

Martin (Student)

• "But I choose what I follow. Isn't it my responsibility?"
• "What about people who post hateful content anonymously?"
• "Doesn't blocking and reporting solve the problem?"

Segment 3 — The Big Question

User vs. Platform: Who Is Responsible?

~10 minutes

Alinggo

"This is the central debate: who is more responsible for online safety? The user who needs to be careful? Or the platform that shows the content in the first place?"

Alinggo

"Right now, we mostly tell users: 'Block the person. Report the content.' But we can also ask: Why does harmful content appear again and again? Why doesn't the platform stop it earlier? That's the shift from personal protection to systemic change."

💡 Padlet activity simulation

In practice: Martin picks a side ("I think the user is more responsible because..." or "I think the platform is more responsible because...") and defends it. Alinggo facilitates, asking probing questions without taking a side. Practice managing a debate, not winning it.

Segment 4 — Closing

Change the System, Not Just the User

~5 minutes

Alinggo

"We need systemic change, not just personal protection. Don't just fix the problem — change the system. Ask: why is the system allowing this to happen?"

Digital literacy debates get opinionated. These scenarios practice staying structural.

Scenario 1
"It's the Internet — You Should Expect It"
A student normalizes online harassment.
Student (Martin)

"The internet has always been like this. If you can't handle it, just log off."

What to practice

"That's a common perspective. But think about it this way: if a building keeps collapsing, do we tell people 'don't go inside' — or do we fix the building? 'Just log off' puts all the responsibility on the victim and none on the platform that profits from the content. The internet isn't a natural phenomenon — it's designed by people. And what's designed can be redesigned."

Scenario 2
"But Free Speech!"
A student invokes free expression against content moderation.
Student (Martin)

"If platforms start removing content, that's censorship. What about free speech?"

What to practice

"Important question. But here's the distinction: we're not asking platforms to silence opinions. We're asking them to stop amplifying harm. There's a difference between allowing someone to post something and actively pushing it to millions of feeds. Free speech doesn't mean free amplification. The algorithm is an editorial choice — and editorial choices should come with accountability."

Scenario 3
Explaining "Algorithm" to a Non-Technical Student
A student needs a simple explanation.
Student (Martin)

"What exactly is an 'algorithm'? I hear the word everywhere but I don't get it."

What to practice

"Think of the algorithm as an invisible DJ at a party. You didn't choose the music — the DJ did. But the DJ watches which songs make you dance, and plays more songs like those. The problem is: if you 'dance' to a shocking video — even by watching it for just 5 seconds longer — the DJ plays you more shocking videos. The algorithm doesn't care if the content is good for you. It only cares if you stay at the party."

Scenario 4
Facilitating the User vs. Platform Debate
Practice managing a classroom debate without taking a side.
The challenge

Martin argues "the user is responsible." Alinggo must: (1) paraphrase his argument fairly, (2) present the counter-argument without dismissing his point, (3) ask a deepening question, and (4) invite other perspectives. Practice this facilitation sequence. The goal is not to win — it's to make both sides think harder.
