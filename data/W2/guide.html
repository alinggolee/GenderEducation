Week 2 Teaching Practice

Gender Education · Alinggo S.L. Lee

Warm up the W2 vocabulary and sentence frames. This week's theme is naming as gendered politics — names carry expectations, and those expectations shape opportunity.

Vocabulary Quick-Fire

Tap each word to reveal. Practice saying the example aloud as if explaining to a student — don't just read it.

Socialization (n.)
Definition: The process by which we learn the values, norms, and behaviors considered acceptable in society.
Try saying: "Naming is one of the earliest forms of gender socialization — it happens before a child can even speak."
社會化 — 學習社會規範和行為的過程。命名是最早期的性別社會化之一。
Patrilineal (adj.)
Definition: Relating to descent or inheritance traced through the father's line.
Try saying: "In the UK and US, surnames are typically inherited patrilineally — meaning children take the father's last name."
父系的 — 沿著父親一方傳承。台灣過去也以父姓為主。
Symbolism (n.)
Definition: The use of symbols to represent ideas or qualities beyond their literal meaning.
Try saying: "Names carry cultural symbolism — for example, naming a girl '淑' suggests gentleness, while naming a boy '偉' implies greatness."
象徵主義 — 用符號來表達超越字面意義的想法或品質。
Gendered Naming (n. phrase)
Definition: The practice of choosing names based on gender expectations — embedding assumptions about a child's role before they can choose for themselves.
Try saying: "Bi Heng-Da calls this 'gendered naming' — parents project their expectations onto children through the names they give."
性別化命名 — 畢恆達（2007）指出命名是「性別政治」的一環。
Social Perception (n.)
Definition: How other people see and evaluate you, often based on surface-level cues like appearance, accent, or name.
Try saying: "Names structure social perception — research shows that names can influence evaluations in contexts like hiring."
社會知覺 — 他人如何看待和評價你，通常基於表面線索。

Sentence Frame Drill

Martin gives a topic (e.g., "Taiwanese girl names with 淑 or 婷," "English surnames," "indigenous Paiwan names"), and Alinggo responds using these frames.

Frame 1 — Making a Comparison:
"While some cultures focus on _____, others emphasize _____ in their names."
Example: "While some cultures focus on birth order, others emphasize patrilineal heritage in their names."
Frame 2 — Analyzing a Pattern:
"In this example, the name reinforces a gender stereotype by suggesting that..."
Example: "...girls should be gentle and elegant, while boys should be strong and ambitious."
Frame 3 — Synthesizing a Concept:
"Therefore, naming is not just personal; it is a powerful tool of..."
Example: "...gender socialization that shapes expectations before a child can even speak."

💡 Drill idea: Martin names a naming tradition (Japanese names, Spanish double surnames, Islamic naming, Paiwan names) → Alinggo uses all 3 frames to build a 60-second comparison. Repeat 3–4 times.

Alinggo delivers the W2 lesson: Gender Concepts & Naming. Martin plays the international student. The lesson moves from concept → personal reflection → cross-cultural analysis.

⏱ Suggested total time 30 min
Segment 1 — Opening Hook

Your Name Is Not Neutral

~5 minutes

Alinggo (Teacher)

"Good morning. Before we begin — I want everyone to do something. Close your eyes and say your name quietly to yourself."

Alinggo

"Now ask yourself: does it feel like a perfectly tailored suit that helps you move through the world? Or does it feel more like a tight label that was attached to you before you had a choice?"

你的名字像是量身訂做的西裝，還是別人貼上的標籤？
Alinggo

"Names are not just sounds. Scholar Bi Heng-Da argues that naming is a form of gendered politics. Parents project their expectations onto children before those children can speak, walk, or choose for themselves."

💡 Teaching tip

The "close your eyes" moment creates intimacy. Give it a full 5-second pause. Don't rush into the theory — let the personal experience arrive first.

Segment 2 — Core Concept

Naming as Gendered Politics

~8 minutes

Alinggo

"Let me give you some concrete examples. In English-speaking cultures, the name 'Grace' implies elegance. 'Edward' means 'guardian of wealth.' Even in English, names carry gendered expectations."

Alinggo

"Here in Taiwan, we see similar patterns. Girls' names are often 'soft' — think of characters like 淑、婷、雅. Boys' names tend to be 'strong' — 偉、志、豪. These are not random choices. They are social signals."

台灣女生名字常用「淑、婷、雅」，男生常用「偉、志、豪」——這些都不是巧合。
Alinggo

"And names don't just affect how you feel about yourself — they affect how others treat you. Research shows that names influence evaluations in hiring, education, and social settings."

Martin (Student) — prompt

Try asking: "But isn't a name just a name? My parents just liked how it sounded." This gives Alinggo a chance to push back gently with the socialization argument.

Segment 3 — Cross-Cultural Analysis

Indigenous Names & Naming Erasure

~8 minutes

Alinggo

"Now, let's look at naming from a different angle. In Taiwan, indigenous naming traditions — for example, among the Paiwan people — work very differently. Names reflect connection to land, ancestry, and social role, not just biological traits."

Alinggo

"So here's an important question: when a culture's naming system is erased — for example, through colonization — do the gender roles within that culture change too? Is a name a 'family resume', or is it a 'personal introduction'?"

當一個文化的命名系統被消滅，該文化中的性別角色也會隨之改變嗎？
Martin (Student) — prompt

Respond with a cross-cultural comparison: "In Japan, women traditionally change their surname at marriage. Does that count as naming politics too?" Let Alinggo connect this back to patrilineal systems.

💡 Teaching tip

This is the deepest section. If Alinggo gets stuck in English, switching to Chinese for one key sentence is fine — then rephrase it back in English. Model the 75/25 balance.

Segment 4 — Closing

Names → Signals → Expectations → Opportunity

~4 minutes

Alinggo

"So what have we learned? Names are not destiny. But they are social signals. And signals create expectations. And expectations... shape opportunity."

Alinggo

"Next week, we'll go further. We'll ask: How do we 'perform' those expectations in our daily lives? That's Week 3 — gender expression."

💡 Closing tip

The chain "names → signals → expectations → opportunity" is the thesis. Say it slowly, with pauses between each word. Let the logic build. This is the sentence students should remember.

Classroom scenarios specific to the naming topic. Martin plays the student — Alinggo responds as the teacher, in English.

Scenario 1
"My Name Means Nothing"
A student dismisses the idea that names carry meaning.
Student (Martin)

"I think you're overthinking this. My parents just picked a name they liked. There's no politics in my name."

What to practice

Don't argue — redirect with curiosity. Try: "That's a fair point! But let me ask — if your parents 'just liked it,' why did they like it? What made that particular name sound right for a boy or for a girl? Sometimes the patterns are invisible precisely because they feel so natural."

Scenario 2
The Sensitive Topic
A student reveals their name was changed and feels emotional.
Student (Martin)

"Actually... I changed my name when I was 20 because I hated the one my parents gave me. It always felt wrong."

What to practice

Acknowledge, don't probe. Try: "Thank you for sharing that — it takes courage. And actually, your experience is exactly what we're talking about today. When a name doesn't fit, we can feel it. That gap between the name and the person is where we see the power of gendered naming." Then move on — don't dwell.

Scenario 3
The Cross-Cultural Challenge
A student from a different culture questions whether Taiwan-centric examples apply to them.
Student (Martin)

"These examples are all about Chinese and English names. In my culture, names work completely differently. How is this relevant to me?"

What to practice

Turn the challenge into a learning moment: "Great question — and I'd love to hear about your naming tradition! That's exactly why we study this topic cross-culturally. Can you tell us: in your culture, who decides the name? Are there different expectations for boys' and girls' names? Let's compare." This invites the student to become the expert.

Scenario 4
Explaining "Patrilineal" Simply
A student doesn't understand the word.
Student (Martin)

"Sorry — what does 'patrilineal' mean? I've never heard that word."

What to practice

Break it into parts: "Good question! Let's break it down. 'Patri-' comes from 'father' — like 'paternal.' And '-lineal' means 'line' or 'lineage.' So patrilineal means 'following the father's line.' For example, most children in Taiwan take their father's surname. That's a patrilineal system."

Scenario 5
Facilitating the "Name Swap" Activity
Practice giving instructions for a discussion activity.
The challenge

Alinggo must give clear instructions in English for this activity: "Imagine you swapped names with someone of a different gender today. How would the world treat you differently?" Practice giving the instruction, setting the time limit, and then facilitating the share-back.

Example instruction

"OK, here's what I want you to do. Take one minute and think: if you woke up tomorrow with a name traditionally given to the opposite gender — how would people treat you differently? At work? On a date? In your family? ... OK, who wants to share what they thought?"
