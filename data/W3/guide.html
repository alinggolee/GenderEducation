Week 3 Teaching Practice

Gender Education · Alinggo S.L. Lee

Week 3 is about gender expression as performance — what we show the world, and the social rules behind those choices. This is the most personal week so far.

Vocabulary Quick-Fire

Tap to reveal. Practice explaining each term as if a B1-level student just asked "What does that mean?"

Gender Expression (n.)
Definition: The external presentation of gender through clothing, hairstyle, behavior, voice, and body language.
Try saying: "Gender expression is what you show the world — it's different from identity, which is what you feel inside."
性別表達 — 透過穿著、髮型、行為等外在方式向世界展現你的性別。
Androgynous (adj.)
Definition: Having a combination of both masculine and feminine characteristics in appearance or behavior.
Try saying: "An androgynous style might include short hair with a flowy shirt — blending what society calls 'masculine' and 'feminine.'"
中性的 — 同時結合男性化和女性化的特徵。
Social Reinforcement (n.)
Definition: Using praise, criticism, or social pressure to encourage behavior that matches expected gender norms.
Try saying: "When someone says 'boys don't cry,' that's social reinforcement — it's teaching boys to hide certain emotions."
社會增強 — 用讚美或批評來鼓勵「正確」的性別行為。
Tomboy / Gentleboy (n.)
Definition: Historically reclaimed terms for non-traditional gender expression. "Tomboy" originally meant a rude boy, but is now reclaimed as bold, sporty femininity.
Try saying: "The word 'tomboy' has shifted from an insult to an identity — it's an example of how language evolves with social norms."
男人婆 / 暖男 — 這些詞彙的含義隨著社會而改變。
Social Construction (n.)
Definition: An idea or concept that was created and developed by society, rather than being natural or biological.
Try saying: "What counts as 'masculine' or 'feminine' is a social construction — it changes across cultures and across time."
社會建構 — 由社會創造的概念，而非天生或生物性的。

Sentence Frame Drill

Martin gives a prompt (e.g., "a boy wearing nail polish," "David Bowie's style," "Lin Yu-Ting"), and Alinggo builds a response using these analysis frames.

Frame 1 — Making a Claim:
"I believe gender expression is a social construction because..."
Example: "...what's considered 'feminine' clothing in one culture is completely normal for men in another."
Frame 2 — Describing a Style:
"They express _____ through _____ , which challenges the traditional expectation that..."
Example: "They express masculinity through softness and empathy, which challenges the expectation that men must be tough."
Frame 3 — Explaining the Difference:
"It's important to remember that gender expression is external, whereas identity is..."
Example: "...an internal feeling. A person's clothing doesn't tell you who they are inside."

💡 Drill idea: Martin shows an example (real or hypothetical — "a man in a skirt in Scotland," "a woman boxer," "a K-pop idol") → Alinggo uses all 3 frames for a 90-second analysis. Challenge: can she do it without pausing for more than 3 seconds?

Alinggo delivers the W3 lesson: Gender Expression as Performance. This is the most discussion-heavy week — lots of student interaction.

⏱ Suggested total time 30 min
Segment 1 — Opening

What Did You Put On This Morning?

~5 minutes

Alinggo (Teacher)

"Good morning. I want to start with a simple question: What did you put on this morning? Not just your clothes — your hairstyle, your posture, your way of speaking. All of that is your gender expression."

Alinggo

"Last week, we talked about how names carry expectations. This week, we go further. We're going to talk about how we perform those expectations — every single day, through our bodies and our presentation."

Alinggo

"Here's the key idea for today: gender expression is not about who you are. It's about what you show. And what counts as 'masculine' or 'feminine' is not biology — it's culture."

性別表達不是「你是誰」，而是「你展現什麼」。什麼是「男性化」或「女性化」不是生物學，而是文化。
💡 Teaching tip

The opening question is casual and disarming. Gesture at your own outfit as you say it. It breaks the formality and makes the concept immediately concrete.

Segment 2 — Core Concept

Expression ≠ Identity + The Genderbread Model

~8 minutes

Alinggo

"Let me introduce an important model: the Genderbread Person. It shows us that gender has several independent components — identity, expression, sex, and orientation. They're all separate."

Alinggo

"Today we're focusing on expression. Expression is external — it's your clothing, your hairstyle, your posture, your speech. Identity is internal — it's how you feel. A person can express femininity but identify as male. These are independent."

Alinggo

"And here's the critical point: what society considers 'masculine' or 'feminine' expression is a social construction. It changes across cultures. It changes across time. High heels were originally invented for men. Pink was once a boy's color."

高跟鞋最初是為男性設計的，粉紅色曾被認為是男孩的顏色。「男性化」和「女性化」不是固定的。
Martin (Student) — prompt

Ask: "Wait, high heels were for men? Are you serious?" Let Alinggo elaborate — this is always a great attention-grabber.

💡 Comprehension check

Ask: "So — can someone tell me: if a woman has short hair and wears a suit, what does that tell us about her gender identity?" The answer is: nothing, necessarily. Expression ≠ identity.

Segment 3 — Discussion

Icons, Anxiety & the Imaginary Gender Police

~12 minutes

Alinggo

"Let's look at some real examples. Think about David Bowie — his glam rock style in the 1970s mixed 'masculine' rock performance with makeup and dresses. Or think about Lin Yu-Ting, whose powerful presence challenged what people expected a female athlete to look like."

Alinggo

"Now I want to ask you something personal — and you don't have to share if you don't want to. Have you ever felt a spark of anxiety when wearing something that didn't match other people's expectations?"

Alinggo

"If yes — where did that anxiety come from? Was it from you? Or was it from the 'imaginary gender police' in the room — the feeling that someone is watching and judging?"

那個焦慮來自你自己，還是來自房間裡「想像中的性別警察」——那種被人注視和評判的感覺？
Martin (Student) — response prompts

Try different student types:
• Open: "Actually, I once wore a pink shirt and my friends teased me all day."
• Resistant: "I just wear whatever. I don't think about gender when I get dressed."
• Curious: "What about K-pop idols? They wear makeup but nobody calls them feminine."

💡 Discussion bridge — Tomboy / Gentleboy

After the personal discussion, transition to language: "Let's talk about labels. Words like 'tomboy' have changed meaning over time. Originally it meant a 'rude boy' — but now it's reclaimed as bold, sporty femininity. In your social circle, is it 'braver' for a girl to be masculine, or for a boy to be feminine?"

Segment 4 — Closing

Your Body Is Your Canvas

~5 minutes

Alinggo

"Let me leave you with this thought. Gender expression is not a binary choice. It's not 'masculine OR feminine.' It's a flowing spectrum."

Alinggo

"You don't have to shrink yourself to fit someone else's script. Your body is your canvas. And your expression is the first story you tell the world — every single day."

💡 Closing tip

This is a powerful final image. Say it slowly. Make eye contact. Let the last sentence ("every single day") hang in the air for a beat. That's how good teachers land a closing.

Week 3 gets personal. These scenarios practice the trickiest moments in a gender expression discussion.

Scenario 1
"Isn't That Just Being Gay?"
A student conflates expression with orientation.
Student (Martin)

"So if a boy wears makeup and dresses femininely, doesn't that mean he's gay?"

What to practice

This is common and must be handled calmly. Try: "That's a very common assumption, and it's good that you're asking. But actually — no. Gender expression is about how you present yourself. Sexual orientation is about who you're attracted to. They're separate. A man who wears makeup might be straight, gay, bisexual, or anything else. The clothes don't tell us about attraction."

Scenario 2
The "I Don't Care" Student
A student says they never think about gender expression.
Student (Martin)

"Honestly, I just wear whatever is comfortable. I don't think about gender when I get dressed. This topic doesn't apply to me."

What to practice

Flip the perspective: "That's interesting — and I actually think it proves the point. The fact that you can 'not think about it' might be because your expression already matches what society expects. For people whose natural expression doesn't match expectations, they can't 'not think about it.' That's what we call privilege — and I don't mean that as an insult. It's just a pattern worth noticing."

Scenario 3
Explaining "Social Construction" Simply
A student looks confused by the term.
Student (Martin)

"You keep saying 'social construction.' Can you explain that in simple English?"

What to practice

Use a concrete example: "Sure! Think about it this way. In Scotland, men wear kilts — which look like skirts. In Korea, men wear BB cream — which is like makeup. Are they less 'masculine'? No — because what counts as masculine is different in different places. That's what 'social construction' means: society built these rules, not nature. And different societies built different rules."

Scenario 4
The Double Standard Question
A student notices the asymmetry in how expression is policed.
Student (Martin)

"It seems like girls can wear pants and no one cares, but if a boy wears a skirt, everyone freaks out. Why is that?"

What to practice

This is an excellent observation to affirm and build on: "That's a really sharp observation! You've noticed an asymmetry. In many societies, it's more accepted for girls to express masculinity than for boys to express femininity. Why? Because historically, masculine traits are valued more highly. So a girl 'moving up' toward masculinity is acceptable, but a boy 'moving down' toward femininity feels threatening. This tells us something about power, not just about clothes."

Scenario 5
Handling a Personal Disclosure
A student shares something vulnerable about their own expression.
Student (Martin)

"Actually, I've always wanted to try wearing more colorful or 'feminine' clothes, but I'm afraid of what my family would say."

What to practice

Honor the courage, don't give advice. Try: "Thank you for sharing that. It takes real courage to say that out loud. And what you're describing — that gap between what you want to express and what you feel safe expressing — is exactly what we're studying in this class. You're not alone in that experience. Many people feel it. And noticing that gap is the first step." Don't probe further. Move on naturally.
