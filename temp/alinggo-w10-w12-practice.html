Weeks 10–12 Teaching Practice

Gender Education · Alinggo S.L. Lee

Week 10 tackles the most serious topic so far: sexual harassment as a boundary crossing within unequal systems. The Three-Part Frame (Physical → Emotional → Power) is the analytical backbone. Teaching tone: compassionate, precise, never sensational.

Vocabulary Quick-Fire

Consent (n.)
Definition: Permission or agreement for something to happen — must be free, informed, and ongoing.
Try saying: "Consent is not just the absence of 'no.' It is the presence of a clear, enthusiastic 'yes.'"
同意 — 必須是自由的、知情的、持續的。不是沒有說「不」就算同意。
Power Imbalance (n.)
Definition: A situation where one person has significantly more power or influence than another — making true consent difficult.
Try saying: "In this case, the power imbalance between a professor and a student made real consent very difficult."
權力不對等 — 當一方擁有顯著更多的權力或影響力，真正的同意變得困難。
Boundary (n.)
Definition: A personal limit — physical, emotional, or digital — that defines what is acceptable to you.
Try saying: "Sexual harassment is not a misunderstanding. It is a boundary crossing within an unequal system."
界線 — 身體、情感或數位上的個人底線，定義了什麼對你來說是可接受的。
Accountability (n.)
Definition: Being responsible for your actions and their consequences — applies to individuals AND institutions.
Try saying: "We must ask institutions to take more accountability — not just punish individuals, but change the systems that allow harm."
問責 — 為自己的行為及其後果負責。適用於個人和制度。
Harassment (n.)
Definition: Unwanted behavior that violates a person's dignity or creates an intimidating, hostile, or degrading environment.
Try saying: "Harassment is not a minor dispute. It is a systemic issue sustained by power, silence, and social norms."
騷擾 — 侵犯個人尊嚴或製造恐嚇、敵意、貶低環境的不受歡迎行為。

Sentence Frame Drill

Frame 1 — Making a Claim:
"I believe institutional responsibility is important to prevent harm because..."
Frame 2 — Describing a Case:
"In this case, the power imbalance made real consent difficult because..."
Frame 3 — Proposing Action:
"If we focus on power, not self-blame, we can build a safer culture by..."

💡 Three-Part Frame drill: Martin describes a scenario. Alinggo must analyze it through: (1) Physical — what happened? (2) Emotional — how did it feel? (3) Power — who holds authority? Practice 3 rounds.

W10: Sexual Harassment — Deconstructing the System of Harm. This lesson requires the most careful facilitation. Ground rules first, Tea Consent video as anchor, then the paradigm shift from "why didn't you say no?" to "what was the power gap?"

⏱ Suggested total time30 min
Segment 1 — Opening & Ground Rules

This Topic Requires Extra Care

~5 minutes

Alinggo

"Today's topic is sexual harassment. Before we begin, I want to set some ground rules. This is an academic discussion. We will use precise language. We will not share personal stories unless you choose to. And we will focus on systems, not on judging individuals."

Alinggo

"Here is the key idea for today: sexual harassment is not a minor personal dispute. It is a boundary crossing within an unequal system. To understand it, we need to look at three things: what happened physically, how it felt emotionally, and who holds the power."

性騷擾不是小小的個人糾紛，而是不平等系統中的越界行為。我們用三個層面分析：身體、情感、權力。
💡 Teaching tip

Remind students: "If at any point this discussion feels too heavy, you are welcome to step out. Your wellbeing comes first." This isn't just polite — it's ethical facilitation.

Segment 2 — Tea Consent

The Choice in the Cup of Tea

~8 minutes

Alinggo

"Let's start with a simple analogy. Imagine I offer you a cup of tea. If you say 'yes, please!' — great, I'll make you tea. If you say 'no thanks' — I should not pour tea down your throat. If you say 'maybe' — I should not force you to drink. And if you fall asleep — I definitely should not pour tea into your unconscious mouth."

Alinggo

"This is the concept of consent. It sounds simple with tea, right? But in real life, when your body gives you a 'bad vibe' — a chill, a tightness in your stomach — do you trust that feeling? Or do you talk yourself out of it?"

Martin (Student)

• "But what if someone says yes at first and then changes their mind?"
• "What if you're not sure if the other person is uncomfortable?"
• Ask: "Can consent be given if one person has much more power?"

💡 Key point

Consent can be withdrawn at any time. It must be ongoing, not a one-time checkbox. Link back to the "power imbalance" concept: when one person holds authority, "yes" might not be freely given.

Segment 3 — The Paradigm Shift

From "Why Didn't You Say No?" to "What Was the Power Gap?"

~10 minutes

Alinggo

"Here is the most important shift in this entire lesson. When someone is harassed, people often ask: 'Why didn't you say no? Why didn't you fight back?' This puts the responsibility on the victim."

Alinggo

"But what if we ask different questions? What if we ask: 'What was the power gap between these two people?' And: 'What kind of environment allowed this person to feel they could cross a boundary?'"

不要問「你為什麼不說不？」要問「這兩個人之間的權力差距是什麼？」「什麼樣的環境讓這個人覺得可以越界？」
Alinggo

"This shift changes everything. It moves from blaming the individual to examining the system. And that's where real prevention begins."

Martin (Student)

Challenge: "But don't people have a responsibility to protect themselves?" — This gives Alinggo practice holding the structural frame without dismissing personal agency.

Segment 4 — Closing

Boundaries, Not Misunderstandings

~5 minutes

Alinggo

"Sexual harassment is not about misunderstanding. It is about boundaries crossing within unequal systems. When we understand the system, we can change it."

💡 Padlet activity

In a real class, Alinggo would direct students to a Padlet for reflection. In practice, Martin can simply write a one-sentence reflection and share it aloud. Alinggo responds with a brief, respectful acknowledgment.

These scenarios practice the hardest facilitation moments in a harassment discussion. The key skill: redirecting victim-blame toward structural analysis without shaming the student who asks.

Scenario 1
"But She Was Wearing..."
A student implies clothing plays a role.
Student (Martin)

"But if someone dresses provocatively, aren't they partly responsible for what happens?"

What to practice

Stay calm, redirect to structure: "I understand why that feels logical. But let me ask: does what someone wears give another person permission to touch them? The answer is no. Clothing is expression — we covered that in Week 3. Harassment is about power and boundaries. No outfit is an invitation. The responsibility lies with the person who crosses the boundary, and the system that allows it."

Scenario 2
"It Was Just a Joke"
A student normalizes a common excuse.
Student (Martin)

"Sometimes people just say things as a joke. Not everything is harassment. We're being too sensitive."

What to practice

"That's a very common perspective, and I appreciate you raising it. But remember our three-part frame. What matters isn't the intention — it's the impact. 'Just a joke' is actually one of the key social norms that allows harassment to persist. When we excuse behavior with humor, we normalize boundary crossing. The question isn't 'did they mean it?' — it's 'did it cross a boundary?'"

Scenario 3
Walking Through the Three-Part Frame
Practice applying the framework to a hypothetical case.
Student (Martin)

"A senior colleague keeps putting their hand on a junior colleague's shoulder and calling them 'sweetie.' The junior feels uncomfortable but doesn't say anything. Can you analyze this?"

What to practice

Walk through each lens:
"Physical: unwanted touching and an overly familiar nickname. Emotional: the junior feels uncomfortable but stays silent — why? Because of the third lens: Power. The senior has authority — career influence, seniority, social capital. The junior's silence isn't consent — it's a survival response in an unequal system. This is why we must ask 'what's the power gap?' instead of 'why didn't you say no?'"

Scenario 4
A Student Gets Emotional
Someone is clearly affected by the discussion.
What to practice

Don't probe, don't draw attention. Possible response: "I can see this topic resonates with some of you. That's completely normal — these are real issues that affect real people. Remember, you don't have to share anything personal. If you need a moment, that's absolutely fine." Then continue the lesson naturally. After class, offer: "If you'd like to talk or need support, I'm here." Don't push.

Session Notes

Week 11 shifts from visible power (harassment) to invisible power — how beauty standards discipline our bodies through internalization, not force. The thesis: "Harassment uses force; beauty uses mirrors."

Vocabulary Quick-Fire

Aesthetic Hierarchy (n.)
Definition: A ranking system of appearance — where some body types, skin colors, or features are valued more than others.
Try saying: "This advertisement reinforces an aesthetic hierarchy by only showing thin, light-skinned models."
審美階層 — 一套外貌的排序系統，某些體型、膚色或特徵被賦予更高價值。
Body Surveillance (n.)
Definition: The constant monitoring and evaluation of one's own body against social standards — we become both the watcher and the watched.
Try saying: "Social media intensifies body surveillance — we check ourselves in every photo, every filter, every selfie."
身體監視 — 持續以社會標準監控和評估自己的身體。我們成為監視者也是被監視者。
Commodification (n.)
Definition: Turning something natural (a body, a feature, an insecurity) into a product that can be sold for profit.
Try saying: "The commodification of the body transforms natural features into profit opportunities — insecurity becomes a business model."
商品化 — 將自然事物（身體、特徵、不安全感）轉化為可出售牟利的商品。
Self-Regulation (n.)
Definition: Disciplining yourself — through makeup, clothing, diet, exercise — to conform to social gender norms without anyone directly telling you to.
Try saying: "Self-regulation is when we discipline our own bodies to meet gender expectations — nobody forces us, but the pressure is everywhere."
自我規訓 — 透過化妝、服裝、飲食等方式自我約束，以符合社會性別規範。
Internalization (n.)
Definition: When external social norms become your own internal beliefs — you start believing the standards are your own idea.
Try saying: "Internalization is when you believe 'I should be thinner' — not because someone told you directly, but because the message was repeated so many times it became your inner voice."
內化 — 當外在的社會規範變成你內在的信念，你開始相信那些標準是自己的想法。

Sentence Frame Drill

Frame 1:
"This phenomenon illustrates how beauty standards function as a form of gender regulation..."
Frame 2:
"From a structural perspective, this standard is socially constructed to benefit..."
Frame 3:
"The media reinforces body surveillance by constantly focusing on..."

W11: Body & Beauty Standards — From Regulation to Discipline. The bridge from W10: harassment uses force; beauty uses mirrors. Both are about control.

⏱ Suggested total time30 min
Segment 1 — Opening

The Invisible Cage

~5 minutes

Alinggo

"Last week we talked about harassment — visible power, clear violations. This week we move to something subtler: invisible power. Beauty standards don't force you. They whisper. And the whisper says: 'You are not enough.'"

Alinggo

"Here's a question: How much time do you spend daily monitoring your appearance — checking mirrors, adjusting hair, choosing clothes to meet social expectations? That time? That's the invisible cage."

你每天花多少時間監控自己的外表？那就是「隱形的牢籠」。
Segment 2 — Core Concepts

Shrink vs. Expand & the Profit of Shame

~10 minutes

Alinggo

"Under the aesthetic hierarchy, women are told to shrink — be thin, be soft, take up less space. Men are told to expand — be tall, be muscular, be big. Both groups are controlled, but in different ways."

Alinggo

"And here's the crucial question: who benefits when you feel your natural body is 'not enough'? Industries. Beauty products, cosmetic surgery, fitness supplements, diet programs — insecurity is profitable."

Alinggo — the Kate Winslet case

"Let's talk about Kate Winslet. After Titanic, the media attacked her body. She was called 'too fat' for Hollywood. But she fought back and became an advocate for body diversity. The question is: why does society care so much about a woman's body? And when she fights back — what does she face?"

Martin (Student)

• "But wanting to look good is just human nature, isn't it?"
• "Is there a difference between wanting to be healthy and wanting to fit a beauty standard?"
• "What about men? Are they also affected by beauty pressure?"

Segment 3 — Discussion

#LikeAGirl & Self-Surveillance

~10 minutes

Alinggo

"The short film #LikeAGirl asks people to 'run like a girl' and 'throw like a girl.' Adults perform it as weak and silly. But young girls perform it with full power. At what age does 'like a girl' become an insult?"

Alinggo

"In the age of social media and filters, we become both the 'watcher' and the 'watched.' We surveil ourselves through the imagined eyes of others. That's self-surveillance — and it's exhausting."

💡 Discussion question

"Can we imagine a media representation that values health and diversity over a strict aesthetic hierarchy? What would that look like?"

Segment 4 — Closing

Force vs. Mirrors

~5 minutes

Alinggo

"Harassment uses force. Beauty uses mirrors. Harassment silences through fear. Beauty standards silence through constant comparison. Power is not always loud. Sometimes it whispers: 'You are not enough.' When we recognize that whisper, we reclaim our agency."

💡 Closing tip

The parallel structure (force/mirrors, fear/comparison) is poetic and memorable. Deliver each pair as a contrast, with a slight pause between. The final line should land softly — not as a rallying cry, but as a quiet truth.

Body image is deeply personal. These scenarios practice navigating the line between critical analysis and personal sensitivity.

Scenario 1
"Wanting to Look Good Is Natural"
A student sees no problem with beauty standards.
Student (Martin)

"Everyone wants to look good. That's just human nature. Why are you making it political?"

What to practice

"You're right — wanting to feel good about yourself is natural. But there's a difference between personal preference and a system that tells you what 'good' looks like. When 'looking good' means only one body type, one skin color, one standard — that's not nature. That's an aesthetic hierarchy. And the question is: who set the hierarchy, and who profits from your desire to climb it?"

Scenario 2
"Are You Against Plastic Surgery?"
A student wants a personal opinion.
Student (Martin)

"So are you saying plastic surgery is wrong? Shouldn't people be free to do what they want with their bodies?"

What to practice

"This isn't about judging individual choices. Everyone has the right to make decisions about their own body. The structural question is: why do so many people feel their natural body isn't enough? Where does that feeling come from? When we analyze the system, we're not criticizing the person who gets surgery — we're asking why the industry that creates the insecurity is so profitable."

Scenario 3
"Men Have Body Pressure Too"
A male student feels excluded from the conversation.
Student (Martin)

"We keep talking about women. But men face pressure too — to be tall, muscular, to earn money. Where's that discussion?"

What to practice

"Absolutely — and thank you for raising it. The aesthetic hierarchy controls men too, but differently. Women are told to shrink; men are told to expand. Both are forms of discipline. The 'expand' pressure leads to steroid use, overtraining, emotional suppression. This isn't a competition of suffering — it's evidence that the system controls everyone. Different scripts, same cage."

Scenario 4
Explaining "Commodification" Simply
A student needs a simpler explanation.
Student (Martin)

"What does 'commodification of the body' mean?"

What to practice

"Sure. Imagine you have freckles. Freckles are just... freckles. But then a company makes a cream that says 'remove your freckles!' Now your freckles have become a 'problem' — and the solution costs money. That's commodification: taking something natural about your body and turning it into a product to sell. The industry doesn't just sell the cream — it first sells you the insecurity."

Week 12 shifts from bodies to digital systems — how platforms and algorithms amplify gendered harm. The key insight: digital harm is not only caused by bad people; it is caused by how platforms are designed.

Vocabulary Quick-Fire

Algorithm (n.)
Definition: A set of rules a computer follows to decide what you see online — designed to maximize engagement, not safety.
Try saying: "The algorithm prioritized content that got many likes, even if it was harmful — because harmful content drives engagement."
演算法 — 電腦用來決定你看到什麼內容的規則，設計目標是最大化互動，而非安全。
Platform Accountability (n.)
Definition: The responsibility of digital platforms for the content they amplify and the harm their systems enable.
Try saying: "We need more platform accountability — it's not enough to tell users to 'block and report.'"
平台問責 — 數位平台對其放大的內容和系統造成的傷害所應承擔的責任。
Engagement (n.)
Definition: Interaction on social media — likes, shares, comments. Platforms prioritize engagement because it generates data and profit.
Try saying: "Platforms care about engagement because it generates profit — even when that engagement comes from harmful content."
互動 — 社群媒體上的按讚、分享、留言。平台優先追求互動，因為互動產生利潤。
Amplification (n.)
Definition: When a platform's algorithm shows harmful content to more people, making the problem bigger than it would be naturally.
Try saying: "A video that makes fun of girls may get many likes. Then the algorithm amplifies it — showing it to thousands more people."
放大效應 — 演算法將有害內容推送給更多人，使問題規模遠超自然傳播。
Systemic Change (n.)
Definition: Changing the system itself — not just telling individuals to protect themselves, but redesigning how the system works.
Try saying: "We need systemic change, not just personal protection. The question is: why does the system keep allowing this to happen?"
系統性改變 — 不只告訴個人要自我保護，而是改變系統本身的運作方式。

Sentence Frame Drill

Frame 1 — Opening:
"I want to focus on how algorithmic systems affect..."
Frame 2 — Impact:
"Online harm has a disproportionate impact on _____ because..."
Frame 3 — Solution:
"A better strategy would involve structural reform of..."

W12: Digital Gender-Based Violence — How Systems Shape Harm. The lesson moves from platform design → the "digital mirror" → user vs. platform accountability → systemic change.

⏱ Suggested total time30 min
Segment 1 — Opening

It's Not Just Bad People — It's Bad Design

~5 minutes

Alinggo

"Digital harm is not only caused by bad people. It is also caused by how digital platforms are designed. Platforms want you to keep scrolling. Their algorithms show content that gets many likes, comments, and shares. And because of this, shocking or harmful gender content often gets more views."

Alinggo

"Example: a video that makes fun of girls may get many likes. Then the platform shows it to more people. The algorithm amplifies the harm. So the problem becomes bigger — not because more people are bad, but because the system is designed to spread what's engaging, not what's safe."

問題不在於更多人是壞人，而在於系統被設計來傳播有互動的內容，而非安全的內容。
Segment 2 — Discussion

The Digital Mirror & Unequal Impact

~10 minutes

Alinggo

"Have you noticed that your 'For You' page can change how you think about gender? If you often see videos showing girls caring about beauty, you may start thinking that's what girls 'should' do. If you see boys always being strong and not showing feelings, you think that's 'normal.' The algorithm creates a digital mirror that reflects social norms back at you — and reinforces them."

Alinggo

"And digital violence doesn't affect everyone equally. Girls, LGBTQ+ people, and people who look 'different' receive more online attacks. Online problems come from real-life inequality."

Martin (Student)

• "But I choose what I follow. Isn't it my responsibility?"
• "What about people who post hateful content anonymously?"
• "Doesn't blocking and reporting solve the problem?"

Segment 3 — The Big Question

User vs. Platform: Who Is Responsible?

~10 minutes

Alinggo

"This is the central debate: who is more responsible for online safety? The user who needs to be careful? Or the platform that shows the content in the first place?"

Alinggo

"Right now, we mostly tell users: 'Block the person. Report the content.' But we can also ask: Why does harmful content appear again and again? Why doesn't the platform stop it earlier? That's the shift from personal protection to systemic change."

💡 Padlet activity simulation

In practice: Martin picks a side ("I think the user is more responsible because..." or "I think the platform is more responsible because...") and defends it. Alinggo facilitates, asking probing questions without taking a side. Practice managing a debate, not winning it.

Segment 4 — Closing

Change the System, Not Just the User

~5 minutes

Alinggo

"We need systemic change, not just personal protection. Don't just fix the problem — change the system. Ask: why is the system allowing this to happen?"

Digital literacy debates get opinionated. These scenarios practice staying structural.

Scenario 1
"It's the Internet — You Should Expect It"
A student normalizes online harassment.
Student (Martin)

"The internet has always been like this. If you can't handle it, just log off."

What to practice

"That's a common perspective. But think about it this way: if a building keeps collapsing, do we tell people 'don't go inside' — or do we fix the building? 'Just log off' puts all the responsibility on the victim and none on the platform that profits from the content. The internet isn't a natural phenomenon — it's designed by people. And what's designed can be redesigned."

Scenario 2
"But Free Speech!"
A student invokes free expression against content moderation.
Student (Martin)

"If platforms start removing content, that's censorship. What about free speech?"

What to practice

"Important question. But here's the distinction: we're not asking platforms to silence opinions. We're asking them to stop amplifying harm. There's a difference between allowing someone to post something and actively pushing it to millions of feeds. Free speech doesn't mean free amplification. The algorithm is an editorial choice — and editorial choices should come with accountability."

Scenario 3
Explaining "Algorithm" to a Non-Technical Student
A student needs a simple explanation.
Student (Martin)

"What exactly is an 'algorithm'? I hear the word everywhere but I don't get it."

What to practice

"Think of the algorithm as an invisible DJ at a party. You didn't choose the music — the DJ did. But the DJ watches which songs make you dance, and plays more songs like those. The problem is: if you 'dance' to a shocking video — even by watching it for just 5 seconds longer — the DJ plays you more shocking videos. The algorithm doesn't care if the content is good for you. It only cares if you stay at the party."

Scenario 4
Facilitating the User vs. Platform Debate
Practice managing a classroom debate without taking a side.
The challenge

Martin argues "the user is responsible." Alinggo must: (1) paraphrase his argument fairly, (2) present the counter-argument without dismissing his point, (3) ask a deepening question, and (4) invite other perspectives. Practice this facilitation sequence. The goal is not to win — it's to make both sides think harder.
