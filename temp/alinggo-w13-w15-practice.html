Week 13 deepens the digital violence analysis with a real case study and the Body-Emotion-Power Triangle. This is the most case-based week — Alinggo must practice handling graphic themes with academic distance and human care.

Vocabulary Quick-Fire

Coercion (n.)
Definition: Using pressure, threats, or manipulation to force someone to do something — even if they technically "agreed."
Try saying: "When trust becomes leverage for a threat, it turns into coercion — it's not consent anymore."
脅迫 — 利用壓力、威脅或操控迫使他人做某事，即使對方表面上「同意」了。
Manipulation (n.)
Definition: Controlling someone by using emotional or psychological influence in a deceptive way.
Try saying: "The main issue in this story is how manipulation turned trust into a weapon."
操控 — 以欺騙性的情感或心理影響來控制他人。
Perpetuate (v.)
Definition: To make something — usually harmful — continue for a long time.
Try saying: "Bystander silence perpetuates a culture of victim-blaming — it keeps the harm going."
使延續 — 讓某事（通常是有害的）持續很長時間。旁觀者的沉默使責怪受害者的文化延續。
Bystander (n.)
Definition: A person who witnesses harm but does not intervene — their silence is part of the system.
Try saying: "If you saw this incident online, what would prevent you from acting? Bystander silence maintains the harmful system."
旁觀者 — 目睹傷害但未介入的人。他們的沉默是系統的一部分。
Victim-Blaming (n.)
Definition: Holding the victim responsible for the harm done to them — often through questions like "why did you send it?"
Try saying: "Asking 'why did she send the photo?' is victim-blaming. The structural question is: why did he feel entitled to share it?"
責怪受害者 — 讓受害者為加害行為負責。結構性問題是：為什麼加害者覺得自己有權這麼做？

Segment 2 — Case Analysis

The Privacy Violation Story

~10 minutes

Alinggo

"Here's the case: A female student sent a private photo to a male student she met on a dating app — someone she trusted. After a conflict, he shared her private images along with her personal identity online as revenge."

Alinggo — apply the triangle

"Let's apply our framework. Body: Her body was exposed without consent — turned from something private into something public. Emotion: Shame and fear are now weapons — she fears public judgment. Power: He had control over the images, and he used that control after a conflict. Trust became leverage."

Alinggo — the paradigm shift

"Now — the common reaction is: 'She shouldn't have sent the photo.' But let's pause. Is 'she sent it because she trusted him' too simple? How did power and fear operate? The structural question is not 'why did she send it?' but 'what gave him the right to share it?'"

Martin (Student)

• "But she did choose to send it..."
• "What about the Five Don'ts? Aren't those useful?"
• "Would people react differently if the genders were reversed?"

Segment 3 — Beyond "Don'ts" & Bystander Silence

Why Simple Rules Aren't Enough

~10 minutes

Alinggo

"The 'Five Don'ts and Four Do's' are important safety rules. But for university-level awareness, they're not enough. Why? Because they focus on what the victim should do — not on the system that allows the harm."

Alinggo

"And let's talk about the silent majority. If you saw this incident online — someone's private images being shared — what would prevent you from acting? Fear of being involved? Not knowing how to help? Bystander silence maintains the harmful system."

💡 Discussion: Gender reversal

"Would the social judgment change if the genders were reversed — if it were a man's photos being shared by a woman? Why or why not?" This question reveals the gendered double standard in victim-blaming.

These scenarios practice the hardest facilitation moments in a DGBV discussion — victim-blaming redirects, bystander engagement, and holding complexity.

Scenario 1
"She Shouldn't Have Sent It"
The most common victim-blaming response.
Student (Martin)

"I mean, I feel bad for her. But she chose to send the photo. She should have known the risk."

What to practice

"I understand that reaction — it's very common. But let me reframe it. If you lend someone your car and they crash it into a wall on purpose — did you 'choose' the crash? You chose to trust. Trust is not consent to betrayal. The question isn't 'why did she trust him?' — it's 'why did he feel entitled to weaponize that trust?' That's where the structural analysis begins."

Scenario 2
"I Wouldn't Get Involved"
A student admits they'd stay silent as a bystander.
Student (Martin)

"Honestly? If I saw something like that online, I'd just scroll past. It's not my problem."

What to practice

"Thank you for being honest — and you're not alone. Most people feel that way. But let's think about what that silence does structurally. When nobody speaks up, the person who shared the images faces no social consequence. The victim carries all the shame. Silence isn't neutral — it's a choice that maintains the system. You don't have to be a hero. But even small actions — not sharing, not laughing, quietly reporting — can break the cycle."

Scenario 3
"What If the Genders Were Reversed?"
Facilitating the gender reversal thought experiment.
The challenge

Alinggo poses the question: "What if a woman shared a man's private photos after a breakup? Would the reaction be the same?" Then Martin responds as a student. Practice facilitating this conversation — the goal is to reveal the gendered double standard without leading the student to a predetermined answer. Let the student discover the asymmetry themselves.

Scenario 4
Critiquing Safety Rules Without Dismissing Them
Practice the nuance of "important but not enough."
Student (Martin)

"But the Five Don'ts are there to protect people. Why are you criticizing them?"

What to practice

"I'm not criticizing them — they're important starting points. But at the university level, we need to go further. The Five Don'ts tell you how to protect yourself. That's necessary. But they don't ask: why does the system allow this harm? Why is it the victim's job to avoid danger, instead of the perpetrator's responsibility not to cause it? Both levels matter. Individual safety AND structural change."

Session Notes

Week 14 is a shift: from analysis to action. Alinggo teaches how to facilitate the "Ready for an Adventure" board game as a form of advocacy. Facilitation is not just playing — it's carrying an ethical position.

Vocabulary Quick-Fire

Facilitation (n.)
Definition: The act of guiding a group toward shared learning or reflection — centering participants' voices, not the facilitator's opinions.
Try saying: "Good facilitation centers student voices rather than the teacher's opinion."
引導 — 引導團體走向共同學習或反思的行為。以參與者的聲音為中心。
Intersectionality (n.)
Definition: How different identities — gender, age, ethnicity, class — overlap to create unique experiences of vulnerability or privilege.
Try saying: "We must consider intersectionality when explaining why some victims face more bias than others."
交叉性 — 不同身分（性別、年齡、種族、階級）如何交疊，創造獨特的脆弱或特權經驗。
Advocacy (n.)
Definition: Public support for a cause or policy — using your knowledge and voice to push for structural change.
Try saying: "Using this board game in schools is a form of digital gender-justice advocacy."
倡議 — 公開支持某項議題或政策。運用知識和聲音推動結構性改變。
Do No Harm (principle)
Definition: The ethical principle that the emotional safety of participants comes before completing the activity.
Try saying: "In my facilitation practice, I will always prioritize Do No Harm — participant safety comes before finishing the game."
不造成傷害 — 參與者的情感安全優先於完成活動的倫理原則。
Structural Awareness (n.)
Definition: Shifting focus from individual "mistakes" to institutional and algorithmic responsibility.
Try saying: "Our facilitation must center structural awareness — we discuss systems, not just individual blame."
結構意識 — 將焦點從個人「錯誤」轉移到制度和演算法的責任。

Sentence Frame Drill

Frame 1 — Ethics:
"In my future practice, I will focus on Do No Harm and..."
Frame 2 — Mechanics:
"The Strategy Cards are effective because they teach..."
Frame 3 — Impact:
"Our goal is to promote accountability and reduce..."

W14: Advocacy & Educational Design. Alinggo teaches HOW to facilitate the board game — not just play it. The key ethics: Do No Harm, Center Structural Awareness, Promote Accountability.

⏱ Suggested total time30 min
Segment 1 — Opening

Facilitation Is Advocacy

~5 minutes

Alinggo

"We've spent 13 weeks analyzing structures. This week, we act. You are going to learn how to facilitate a board game called 'Ready for an Adventure!' — a tool for digital gender-based violence education."

Alinggo

"But facilitation is not just about explaining rules. It is about carrying an ethical position. When you facilitate this game, you are advocating for a safer, more just digital world. Three principles guide us:"

Alinggo — the three ethics

"1. Do No Harm: Participant emotional safety comes before finishing the game.

2. Center Structural Awareness: We discuss systems, not individual blame.

3. Promote Accountability: We clearly identify who is responsible for digital harm."

三個倫理原則：不造成傷害、以結構意識為核心、促進問責。
Segment 2 — Game Mechanics

Cards, Gems & Strategic Thinking

~10 minutes

Alinggo — explain the components

"The game has three types of elements:

Crisis Cards: These represent risk escalation in digital spaces — like non-consensual sharing of images.

Strategy Cards: Actions you can take — 'Save Evidence,' 'Seek Peer Support,' 'Report to School,' 'Contact SIARC.' These are concrete preventive strategies.

Gems: Knowledge, Friendship, Love, Money — the resources needed to navigate a crisis. Notice: you need multiple types of resources. No single gem is enough."

💡 Teaching moment

"Why does the 'Seek Peer Support' card provide a safety gem? Because relational safety is a real resource. Having someone to talk to isn't just emotional comfort — it's a protective factor against harm."

Segment 3 — Ethical Facilitation Practice

When a Player Starts Victim-Blaming

~10 minutes

Alinggo

"The most important facilitation skill is knowing what to do when a player says something that blames the victim. For example: 'Well, she shouldn't have sent the photo in the first place.'"

Alinggo

"This is where your Digital Gender Ethics Cards become your guide. You don't shame the player. You redirect: 'That's one way to look at it. But our ethics card says we Center Structural Awareness. So let's ask: what in the system allowed this harm to happen? That's the analysis we're practicing.'"

Martin (Student/Player)

Practice: Martin says something victim-blaming during a mock game turn. Alinggo must redirect using the ethics cards — calmly, without shaming, maintaining the learning environment.

Segment 4 — Closing

You Carry an Ethical Position

~5 minutes

Alinggo

"Facilitation is not just about explaining rules. It is about carrying an ethical position. When you facilitate this game, you are advocating for a safer, more just digital world."

💡 Debrief question practice

Practice asking ONE debrief question at the end of the game that ensures students understand digital harm as systemic, not personal. Example: "After playing, what did you notice about who was responsible for the harm — the individual, or the system?" Let the question breathe.

A Player Gets Uncomfortable
The Do No Harm principle in action.
Player (Martin)

Don't spotlight them. Option 1: Address the group generally — "Some of these cards touch on difficult topics. Remember, you can always pass on a card or take a moment." Option 2: If the person clearly wants to stop, say privately and gently — "You don't have to continue with this card. Your comfort matters more than the game." Do No Harm is the first principle.

The final week. This is not a new lesson — it's a synthesis. Alinggo must pull together 14 weeks of concepts into a coherent arc and deliver a closing that honors the journey. The Cycle of Liberation is the framework: Awareness → Reflection → Action → Transformation.

Vocabulary Quick-Fire

Synthesize (v.)
Definition: To combine various concepts into a unified whole — seeing the connections between ideas.
Try saying: "In our final presentation, we synthesized theory and practice through our board game design."
綜合 — 將各種概念結合成統一的整體。看見想法之間的連結。
Structural Literacy (n.)
Definition: The ability to see and analyze systemic patterns of power — the core skill of this entire course.
Try saying: "This course has significantly improved my structural literacy — I can now see the systems behind everyday interactions."
結構識讀 — 看見和分析權力系統性模式的能力。整門課的核心技能。
Advocacy (n.)
Definition: Public support for a cause — using knowledge, empathy, and accountability to drive structural change.
Try saying: "Effective advocacy requires a balance of empathy and accountability."
倡議 — 運用知識、同理心和問責來推動結構性改變。
Transformation (n.)
Definition: A fundamental shift in perspective that inspires new behavior and inspires others toward justice.
Try saying: "You are not leaving this course as the same person who entered. That's transformation."
轉化 — 根本性的觀點轉變，激發新行為並啟發他人追求正義。
Cycle of Liberation (n.)
Definition: The growth cycle: Awareness → Reflection → Action → Transformation. Not a line — a cycle that continues.

15 Weeks, One Journey

~8 minutes

Alinggo

"Weeks 1–4: Concepts & Critical Thinking. We learned to see patterns — in names, stories, media, and expression.
