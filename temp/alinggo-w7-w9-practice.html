Weeks 7–9 Teaching Practice

Gender Education · Alinggo S.L. Lee

Week 7 introduces gender as a system of power operating at three levels: individual, interactional, and structural. Paula Stone Williams' TED Talk is the anchor text.

Vocabulary Quick-Fire

Cisnormativity (n.)
Definition: The assumption that everyone's gender identity matches their sex assigned at birth — the invisible default.
Try saying: "Cisnormativity is the assumption that everyone's gender identity matches their assigned sex — forms that only offer 'male' or 'female' are an example."
順性別常規 — 假設每個人的性別認同都與出生時被指定的性別一致。
Legitimacy (n.)
Definition: The quality of being accepted as valid, professional, or authoritative — often granted automatically based on gender.
Try saying: "Paula Stone Williams found that after her transition, her legitimacy in meetings dropped — not because her expertise changed, but because her gender presentation did."
正當性 — 被接受為有效、專業或權威的品質。常因性別而自動被給予或剝奪。
Structural Literacy (n.)
Definition: The ability to recognize and analyze the systemic patterns of power — seeing beyond individual cases to the structure behind them.
Try saying: "Developing structural literacy is essential for creating a more equitable environment — it means seeing the system, not just the symptom."
結構識讀 — 辨識和分析權力系統性模式的能力。看見結構，不只是表面現象。
Privilege (n.)
Definition: Unearned advantages that come from belonging to a socially dominant group — not about individual effort, but about systemic positioning.
Try saying: "Privilege isn't about not working hard. It's about not having to face extra gendered hurdles in your daily life."
特權 — 因屬於社會主流群體而獲得的非努力所得的優勢。不是關於個人努力，而是系統定位。
Gender Hierarchy (n.)
Definition: The ranking system where some gender identities and expressions are valued more than others — often invisible because it's treated as normal.
Try saying: "Gender hierarchy is not always violent — it is often procedural. It operates through meeting dynamics, hiring decisions, and who gets interrupted."
性別階層 — 某些性別身分和表達被賦予更高價值的排序系統。通常不是暴力的，而是程序性的。

Sentence Frame Drill

Martin gives a scenario (a meeting, a job interview, a public space) → Alinggo analyzes using the three-level framework.

Frame 1 — Identifying a Concept:
"This case study is a clear example of how cisnormativity operates in..."
Frame 2 — Structural Link:
"When we look at the structural level, we see that legitimacy is not always earned by..."
Frame 3 — Final Reflection:
"Developing structural literacy is essential for creating..."

💡 Three-level drill: Martin describes a scenario. Alinggo must identify what's happening at the individual level (identity), the interactional level (how others respond), and the structural level (what the institution does). Practice this layered analysis 3 times.

W7: Gender-Based Privilege, Trans Experience & Structural Literacy. Paula Stone Williams' lived experience anchors the lesson. The three levels of power (individual → interactional → structural) are the analytical backbone.

⏱ Suggested total time30 min
Segment 1 — Opening

Gender Is a System of Authority

~5 minutes

Alinggo

"Welcome to Week 7. Today we move from understanding gender as identity to understanding gender as power. Gender is more than a personal feeling — it is an active system of authority that operates at three levels."

Alinggo — the three levels

"Individual level: Your internal identity — how you see yourself.

Interactional level: How others respond to you — including gender policing, or unearned respect.

Structural level: How institutions distribute authority and credibility. Who is automatically seen as 'competent'?"

個人層面：你如何看待自己。互動層面：他人如何回應你。結構層面：制度如何分配權威和可信度。
💡 Teaching tip

Hold up three fingers as you explain each level. The physical gesture helps students remember the framework.

Segment 2 — Core Story

Paula Stone Williams: "I've Lived as Both"

~10 minutes

Alinggo

"Paula Stone Williams was a successful male pastor. Later in life, she transitioned and became a woman. After her transition, she noticed significant changes: people interrupted her more. Her ideas were not taken seriously. She received less respect in meetings."

Alinggo

"She said she lost male privilege. Her expertise didn't change. Her intelligence didn't change. What changed was her gender presentation — and suddenly, the world treated her differently. This is what we mean by structural power."

Alinggo

"Now imagine this: you walk into a meeting with the same expertise you have now. But suddenly, no one takes you seriously — because of how your gender is perceived. How would that loss of 'unearned authority' affect your confidence and your career?"

想像你帶著同樣的專業走進會議室，但突然沒人認真看待你——因為你的性別呈現改變了。這對你的信心和職涯會有什麼影響？
Martin (Student) — prompts

• "But maybe she just wasn't as confident after her transition?"
• "This sounds like it only applies to trans people. How is this relevant to me?"
• "Can you give an example of privilege that isn't about being trans?"

Segment 3 — Discussion

The "Invisible Default" & Daily Privilege

~10 minutes

Alinggo

"Let's talk about the 'invisible default.' In most workplaces, men are promoted more often than women — even when women have the same skills. In public spaces, men can walk alone at night without much fear. When men speak strongly, they're 'confident leaders.' When women do the same, they're called 'too emotional' or 'too aggressive.'"

Alinggo

"Privilege isn't about not working hard. It's about not having to face extra gendered hurdles. Some people can just 'be themselves' in public. Others have to constantly navigate expectations."

💡 Comprehension check

"Can someone give me an example of gender privilege in Taiwan? Think about workplaces, schools, or public spaces." Let the student think before you offer examples.

Segment 4 — Closing

Structure Is Routine, Not Always Loud

~5 minutes

Alinggo

"Gender norms are not always loud — they are routine. Privilege is not announced — it is assumed. Hierarchy is not always violent — it is often procedural."

Alinggo

"When someone transitions, the procedure changes — and the structure becomes visible. Gender education is not about blame. It is about structural literacy — the foundation of social transformation."

💡 Closing tip

The three parallel phrases (routine/assumed/procedural) have a poetic rhythm. Deliver them with slight pauses between each one. This is a thesis statement disguised as a closing.

Privilege is uncomfortable to discuss. These scenarios practice keeping the conversation analytical rather than accusatory.

Scenario 1
"I Don't Have Privilege — I Worked for Everything"
A student takes the concept personally.
Student (Martin)

"I'm a man, and I've worked incredibly hard to get where I am. Nobody handed me anything. So don't tell me I have 'privilege.'"

What to practice

"I hear you, and I don't doubt that you've worked hard. Privilege doesn't mean your life was easy or that you didn't earn your success. It means that certain obstacles — specific hurdles related to gender — weren't placed in your path. Other people with equal talent and equal effort might face hurdles you never had to think about. Paula's story shows this clearly: same person, same brain, different treatment. That's what we mean by structural."

Scenario 2
"This Is Only Relevant for Trans People"
A student doesn't see the universal relevance.
Student (Martin)

"I get that trans people face challenges. But I'm cisgender. Why do I need to learn about this?"

What to practice

"Paula's experience is valuable precisely because it reveals something that affects everyone. Think of it as a natural experiment: same person, different gender presentation, different treatment. Her story makes the invisible system visible. And that system — who gets interrupted, who gets promoted, who gets believed — affects all of us, cisgender or not. Structural literacy helps everyone."

Scenario 3
Explaining "Cisnormativity" Simply
A student is confused by the term.
Student (Martin)

"What is 'cisnormativity'? That word is really confusing."

What to practice

"Let me break it down. 'Cis-' means 'on the same side' — so 'cisgender' means your gender identity matches the sex you were assigned at birth. 'Normativity' means treating something as the norm — the default. So cisnormativity is the assumption that everyone is cisgender. You see it in forms that only offer 'male' or 'female,' in dress codes, in how bathrooms are labeled. It's not evil — it's just invisible. And once you see it, you can design better systems."

Scenario 4
The Three-Level Analysis Challenge
A student asks for a real-life application of the framework.
Student (Martin)

"Can you show me how the three levels work with an everyday example?"

What to practice

Use a workplace example:
"Sure. Let's take a woman engineer in a meeting. Individual level: she knows she's competent — she has the skills and experience. Interactional level: her male colleagues interrupt her frequently, and her ideas are credited to the man who repeats them later. Structural level: the company's promotion criteria reward 'visibility' and 'assertiveness' — traits the system associates with men. Same person, same talent — but the structure distributes authority unevenly."

Session Notes

Week 8 uses Truku (太魯閣族) Gaya as a lens to understand how every society has invisible regulatory frameworks — the shift is from "viewing interesting cultures" to "performing structural analysis."

Vocabulary Quick-Fire

Gender Norm (n.)
Definition: An informal rule about how men and women should behave — enforced by social expectations, not law.
Try saying: "It is a norm in Truku culture for men to hunt and women to weave — but this tells us about social expectations, not biology."
性別規範 — 關於男女應如何行為的非正式規則，由社會期待而非法律執行。
Social Control (n.)
Definition: The mechanisms a community uses to regulate behavior — through surveillance, praise, punishment, or shame.
Try saying: "Gaya acts as a form of social control through community surveillance — the ancestors are watching."
社會控制 — 社群用來規範行為的機制，透過監視、讚美、懲罰或羞恥。
Authority (n.)
Definition: The power to define what is legitimate, right, or normal — and who gets to decide.
Try saying: "Certain leaders have the authority to define what is legitimate behavior — but who gave them that authority?"
權威 — 定義什麼是正當、正確或正常的權力——以及誰有權決定。
Gaya (n., Truku)
Definition: The Truku ancestral code — a regulatory system of social, moral, and spiritual rules. Not just tradition — it's structure.
Try saying: "Gaya is more than tradition — it is a regulatory system that shapes social expectations, gender roles, and moral judgment."
Gaya（太魯閣族祖訓）— 不只是傳統，而是一套規範社會期待、性別角色和道德判斷的制度體系。
Regulatory Framework (n.)
Definition: A system of rules — written or unwritten — that governs behavior in a society. Every culture has one.
Try saying: "Every society has its own regulatory framework — some are written law, some are religious, and some are invisible family rules."
規範架構 — 治理社會行為的規則系統，成文或不成文。每個文化都有。

Sentence Frame Drill

Frame 1 — Analyzing a System:
"Gaya is more than tradition; it is a regulatory system that shapes..."
Frame 2 — Linking to Theory:
"This reflects structural inequality because the power to define morality is held by..."
Frame 3 — Comparing Experiences:
"Similar to the Truku culture, my own family has invisible norms that..."

💡 Concept Ladder drill: Martin gives a cultural rule. Alinggo must classify it: is it a norm (Level 1), social control (Level 2), or authority (Level 3)? Then connect to Gaya. Do 4 rounds with examples from different cultures.

W8: Gaya as Regulatory Framework. The key shift: from tourism ("look at this interesting culture") to structural analysis ("every society has a Gaya").

⏱ Suggested total time30 min
Segment 1 — Opening

From "Interesting Culture" to Structural Analysis

~5 minutes

Alinggo

"Today we study the Truku people of Taiwan and their concept of Gaya. But I want to be clear: we are NOT doing cultural tourism. We're not here to say 'how interesting!' and move on. We are here to perform structural analysis."

Alinggo

"Because here's the key insight: every society has its Gaya. Some are written law. Some are religious texts. Some are invisible family rules. When we analyze Truku culture, we are learning to see our own structure."

每個社會都有自己的 Gaya。分析太魯閣文化時，我們其實在學習看見自己的結構。
Segment 2 — Core Concept

Norm → Role → Expectation → Regulation

~10 minutes

Alinggo

"In Truku culture, Gaya is the ancestral code — a system of social, moral, and spiritual rules. The traditional requirement that 'men must hunt and women must weave' shows us how gender norms cluster into fixed roles."

Alinggo — walk through the chain

"The process works like this: A norm ('men provide food') becomes a role ('men are hunters'). The role creates an expectation ('a real man hunts'). And the expectation is enforced through regulation — social pressure, community judgment, ancestral consequence."

💡 Activity

Ask Martin (as student) to map this chain for a rule from HIS culture. E.g.: "In my family, women cook the holiday meal." → Norm → Role → Expectation → Regulation (what happens if you break it?).

Segment 3 — Discussion

The Ancestors Are Watching (The Invisible CCTV)

~10 minutes

Alinggo

"Gaya is often framed as ancestral protection. But it can also feel like a surveillance camera. Imagine if every choice you made — what you wore, who you ate with — was being watched by the 'eyes of the ancestors.' Does that feel like a warm hug? Or a heavy camera?"

Alinggo — cross-cultural bridge

"Does your culture have an 'invisible Gaya'? In Taiwan, parents tell children: 'Don't waste food or the God of Thunder will punish you.' In Malawian and Christian contexts, children are taught: 'Don't steal because God is watching.' These are all regulatory systems — different names, same function."

你的文化有「隱形的Gaya」嗎？台灣的雷公、基督教的上帝——不同名稱，相同功能。
Martin (Student) — prompts

• Share a family rule: "In my family, my grandmother always said..."
• Challenge: "But isn't Gaya just religion? How is it different?"
• "Who decides what's sacred and what's shameful in your community?"

Segment 4 — Closing

Structure Is Everywhere

~5 minutes

Alinggo

"Gaya is not just tradition — it is a regulatory system. When we remove the word 'Gaya,' what remains? Family rules, gender expectations, and privilege distribution. Week 8 teaches us: structure is everywhere."

💡 Closing tip

The power of this closing is in the "removal" move: strip away the cultural specificity and the universal structure appears. Pause after "structure is everywhere."

Cultural analysis requires care. These scenarios practice respecting the culture while maintaining analytical rigor.

Scenario 1
"Are You Saying Indigenous Culture Is Sexist?"
A student thinks you're criticizing Truku culture.
Student (Martin)

"It sounds like you're saying Truku culture is sexist because they have fixed roles for men and women."

What to practice

"I can see why it might sound that way, but that's not the point. Every culture has gender norms — Truku, Taiwanese, American, European. The question isn't 'is this culture sexist?' The question is: 'how does this system work, and who holds the power within it?' We're analyzing structure, not judging a culture. And the same analysis applies to our own culture."

Scenario 2
"My Grandmother's Rule" — Making It Personal
Help a student connect Gaya to their own family.
Student (Martin)

"My grandmother used to say 'a good girl doesn't go out after dark.' Is that like Gaya?"

What to practice

"Exactly! That's a perfect example. Your grandmother's rule is a norm. If you followed it, you were a 'good girl' — that's the role. The expectation is that girls should be home and safe. And the regulation? Social judgment — if you broke the rule, people would talk. Now — who decided what 'good' means in that context? That's the authority question. Same structure as Gaya, different wrapping."

Scenario 3
"But Tradition Protects Us"
A student defends traditional norms as beneficial.
Student (Martin)

"Without rules and traditions, society would have no order. Gaya protects the community. What's wrong with that?"

What to practice

"Nothing is inherently wrong with structure — we all need it. The question is: who benefits and who is constrained? Protection and control can look very similar. Gaya might protect community harmony, but it also determines who gets to define harmony. That's where the power analysis comes in. We're not asking 'is Gaya good or bad?' We're asking 'who holds the authority, and what happens to those who don't fit?'"

Scenario 4
Explaining "Regulatory Framework" Simply
A student finds the term too academic.
Student (Martin)

"What do you mean by 'regulatory framework'? Can you say it in simple English?"

What to practice

"Think of it as 'the rulebook of your community' — except it's not written down. It's the set of expectations that tells you how to behave, what to wear, who to marry, how to be a 'good' man or woman. In Truku culture, it's called Gaya. In your family, it might be 'what Mom and Dad always said.' In society, it might be 'what everyone just knows.' The name changes — the function is the same."

Session Notes

The final week brings it all together: the dynamic balance between tradition and change. Culture is not fixed — we participate in its evolution. This week requires Alinggo to hold complexity: honoring tradition while encouraging critical thinking.

Vocabulary Quick-Fire

Dynamic Balance (n.)
Definition: Finding a middle ground between old rules and new life — tradition and change existing in constant negotiation.
Try saying: "Culture operates as a dynamic balance — we can keep traditions, change them, or create new meanings."
動態平衡 — 在舊規則和新生活之間找到中間地帶。傳統與變革持續協商。
Reinterpret (v.)
Definition: To give a new, modern meaning to an old tradition — keeping the value while updating the form.
Try saying: "I want to reinterpret this norm to show that gender equality is a modern cultural value — not a rejection of tradition."
重新詮釋 — 給舊傳統賦予新的現代意義，保留價值但更新形式。
Group Consciousness (n.)
Definition: A shared sense of identity that connects individuals into a community — "we" instead of "I."
Try saying: "The Truku identity shifted from scattered clan definitions to a unified group consciousness — shaped by environmental changes and relocation."
群體意識 — 將個人連結成社群的共同認同感。從「我」到「我們」。
Agency (n.)
Definition: The capacity to act independently and make your own choices within (or against) social structures.
Try saying: "When we understand how social rules work, we gain agency — the power to choose whether to keep, change, or resist those rules."
能動性 — 在社會結構中（或對抗社會結構）獨立行動和做出選擇的能力。
Living Tradition (n. phrase)
Definition: Tradition understood not as a static relic but as something alive — continuously reinterpreted by each generation.
Try saying: "Tradition is not a museum piece. It is a living standard that we can reinterpret to fit modern values while respecting its roots."
活的傳統 — 傳統不是靜態的遺物，而是活的，被每一代人不斷重新詮釋。

Sentence Frame Drill

Frame 1 — Making a Claim:
"I believe that tradition is a dynamic balance between..."
Frame 2 — Describing Change:
"_____ caused a shift in legitimacy for many traditional..."
Frame 3 — Proposing Action:
"I want to reinterpret this norm to show that..."

💡 Final drill: Martin names a tradition (wedding customs, New Year rituals, naming conventions). Alinggo must: (1) describe the tradition, (2) identify the gender norm embedded in it, (3) propose a reinterpretation that keeps the value but updates the form. This is the culminating skill of the entire course.

W9: Tradition, Change & Gendered Identity. This is the course's capstone — everything comes together. The lesson balances respect for tradition with the power of agency.

Segment 1 — Opening

When Rules Fade, What Remains?

~5 minutes

Alinggo

"Last week we saw that structure is everywhere. This week we ask: what happens when structure changes? Social rules can feel like a cage. But when those rules fade, people sometimes experience a loss of standards — a feeling of 'who am I without these rules?'"

Alinggo

"Today we explore the dynamic balance between individuals and systems. We are not just managed by structure — we help it evolve."

今天我們探討個人與系統之間的動態平衡。我們不只被結構管理——我們也參與它的演化。
💡 Teaching tip

This opening acknowledges that losing tradition can feel like loss — not just liberation. That nuance prevents the lesson from feeling one-sided.

Segment 2 — Core Concept

Living Traditions & the Formation of Identity

~10 minutes

Alinggo

"Here's a key idea: tradition is not a static relic. It is a living standard. We can reinterpret it to fit modern values. This is not about destroying tradition — it's about keeping it alive by making it relevant."

Alinggo

"Look at how the Truku identity evolved. It shifted from scattered clan definitions to a unified group consciousness — not because the culture died, but because environmental changes and relocation forced a new kind of togetherness. Identity responded to context."

Martin (Student)

Ask: "Can you give me an example of reinterpreting a tradition? Like a real one, not just theory?" Push Alinggo to ground the concept in a concrete example.

Segment 3 — Discussion

The Fading Voice of the Leader

~10 minutes

Alinggo

"Traditional Truku leaders — the Bukun — once managed harmony and external relations. But modern administrative systems have shifted that authority. The elder's role has changed."

Alinggo

"Think about the elders in your own family. When their 'authority' shifts because the world has changed — does that make you feel liberated, or a little sad for the loss of tradition? Can we find our own voice while still respecting our roots?"

當長輩的「權威」因世界改變而轉移——你覺得是解放，還是有點為傳統的消逝感到悲傷？
Martin (Student) — prompts

• "I feel both — liberated AND sad. Is that OK?"
• "My grandfather's values don't match modern Taiwan at all. Should I just ignore them?"
• "How do you respect tradition without being trapped by it?"

Segment 4 — Closing

How Justice Lives

~5 minutes

Alinggo

"Culture is not fixed. It changes over time and across generations. Each of us is part of this change. When we understand how social rules work, we begin to have choices."

Alinggo

"We can choose to keep traditions, change them, or create new meanings. That is the agency we've been building toward all semester."

Alinggo

"Before we end this course — what is your opinion?"

💡 Closing tip

The final question — "what is your opinion?" — is deliberately open. Let it breathe. Don't fill the silence. In a real classroom, this is the moment students take ownership. In practice, let Martin answer as a student, and then Alinggo wraps: "Thank you. That's exactly the kind of thinking this course was designed to encourage."

Week 9 role-plays practice holding the tension between tradition and change — the hardest balance in the entire course.

Scenario 1
"Tradition Worked Fine — Why Change It?"
A student is resistant to reinterpretation.
Student (Martin)

"My grandparents' marriage worked perfectly with traditional roles. My grandmother was happy. Why do we need to change what works?"

What to practice

"And I'm glad it worked for them — truly. The point isn't that traditional arrangements are wrong. The point is that they should be a choice, not a requirement. If your grandmother chose that role freely and was fulfilled, that's wonderful. But what about someone who doesn't fit that mold? The question isn't 'should we destroy tradition?' — it's 'should tradition have room for everyone?'"

Scenario 2
"I Feel Both — Liberated AND Sad"
A student expresses genuine ambivalence.
Student (Martin)

"When I think about my family's old ways changing, I feel free but also... like something is being lost. Is that normal?"

What to practice

"Absolutely — and that feeling is exactly what 'dynamic balance' means. Change doesn't mean erasure. You can honor the values your elders taught you AND choose to express them differently. Feeling both liberated and sad isn't confusion — it's complexity. And complexity is a sign that you're thinking deeply about this."

Scenario 3
"What Do YOU Think, Teacher?"
A student turns the question back to Alinggo.
Student (Martin)

"You've been asking us what we think all semester. But what's YOUR opinion, teacher?"

What to practice

This is a beautiful capstone moment. Alinggo should share a genuine, measured response: "Fair question. I believe tradition is valuable — but it should serve the people inside it, not the other way around. I think the strongest cultures are the ones that can evolve without losing their soul. And I think every person in this room has the right to find their own balance between the roots they inherit and the life they choose to build."

Scenario 4
The Poster Talk Practice
Practice giving a 2-minute structured oral presentation.
The challenge

Alinggo pretends she's a student giving a poster talk. She picks ONE gender norm from the course (naming, expression, media, parenting, Gaya) and presents in 2 minutes using this structure:

(1) Claim: "I believe that..." (state a position)
(2) Evidence: "This is supported by..." (cite a course concept)
(3) Change: "One way to reinterpret this is..." (propose action)
(4) Reflection: "What I learned is..." (personal takeaway)

Martin gives feedback on clarity, flow, and English accuracy. Repeat with a different topic if time allows.
